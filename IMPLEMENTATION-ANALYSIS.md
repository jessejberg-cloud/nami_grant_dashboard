# Implementing the suite at a small, under-resourced, high-pressure nonprofit

*2026-10-05. Written for Jesse and NAMI Southeast Wisconsin leadership. It asks one question of every part of the suite: will it survive contact with an office where everyone is busy, nobody is the computer person, and a new tool has to pay for itself in its first week?*

## The organization this has to work in

Assumptions, stated so they can be corrected: about thirty staff, most wearing several hats; many small events a year and a few large ones; a Microsoft 365 office with no in-house IT; grants managed by one or two people alongside other work; volunteers coordinated by one person; an Executive Director whose attention is the scarcest resource in the building; turnover that means any tool must be learnable without the person who introduced it. The builder is outside the organization, with his own practice, and is the only person who can change the code today.

In an office like this a tool is not adopted because it is good. It is adopted because it removes a task someone already dreads, and it is dropped the first week it adds one. Every judgment below follows from that.

## What is in the suite's favor

- **Nothing to install, nothing to configure.** One link, and the program works. For Radar and the Dashboard there is not even a sign-in. This is the single biggest predictor of adoption in a small office.
- **Only a name is ever required.** A coordinator can type "Resource Fair" between two phone calls and finish later. Tools that demand a complete form at creation die in offices like this.
- **Saves as you go, Undo everywhere, Recently deleted.** The fear that stops staff touching a new system is breaking something. These remove it.
- **It teaches itself.** A welcome, a tour and one-line tips mean no training session has to be scheduled, which in practice means it would never be scheduled.
- **Plain words, phone-friendly, calm.** Staff under pressure skim. There is nothing here that punishes skimming: no "overdue", no red, no counters of what was not done.
- **The reports are the ones a small nonprofit actually needs.** Volunteer hours between two dates, ready for a grant match or an in-kind valuation; money promised versus received; one next step on each grant.
- **It costs nothing and it can stand still safely.** Radar and the solo Volunteers & Events copy are single files that run with no server. If the builder disappeared tomorrow, they would keep working unchanged.

## The risks that are specific to this kind of organization

**1. Adoption dies at the second step.** The first event gets entered with enthusiasm. Nobody marks who came. Hours never add up, the report is empty, and the tool is quietly judged useless. *Mitigation:* one named owner per program, not the Executive Director, and one five-minute routine tied to an existing moment: "the day after an event, mark who came." The Home page's single gentle reminder is built for exactly this, but a reminder on a page nobody opens is nothing. The routine has to live in a person's week.

**2. It becomes a third place to type the same thing.** Volunteer names already live in a spreadsheet, an email list or a sign-up sheet; donors in a donor database or Excel; grant dates in Outlook. A small office cannot afford double entry, and the suite loses that fight every time. *Mitigation:* decide, in writing, what each program replaces. Volunteers & Events replaces the event sign-up sheet and the hours spreadsheet. The Dashboard replaces the grant-deadlines list, wherever it is. Radar replaces the "grants to look at" email folder. If gifts are already in a donor database, use the suite's gifts only for event-tied and in-kind gifts, or not at all. "Add many at once" and the spreadsheet exports are the bridges in and out; use them rather than retyping.

**3. One person can change the code.** Everything that is not a bug fix waits for the builder, and if he is unavailable the suite is frozen. Frozen is survivable (see above), but it is a real dependency. *Mitigation:* phase two names a second person with access, even if they never use it. The programs are single files with the guides beside them, chosen so that a future helper can read them without a build system. Keep it that way: resist rebuilding any of them in a framework.

**4. Two of the three run on young products.** Claude pages and ChatGPT Sites are new, change often, and could change terms or disappear. *Mitigation:* the weekly backup file, which takes ten seconds and is the whole disaster plan for phase one; and phase two, which moves everything to boring, durable hosting that NAMI owns.

**5. Feedback never comes.** Busy people do not write up what confused them; they stop using the thing. *Mitigation:* make feedback cost one line. The two check-ins ask three questions each, by email, and "haven't looked yet" is a complete answer that nobody is chased for. The fastest signal is not words but records: if after two weeks there are no real events or grants in the programs, that is the feedback.

**6. Leadership bandwidth.** The Executive Director cannot be the gatekeeper for access, feedback and decisions. *Mitigation:* the only asks of the Executive Director are forwarding three emails and naming one internal person. Everything else routes to that person and to the builder. If naming someone stalls, propose a name rather than wait: the office manager or the volunteer coordinator, as a role, not a project.

**7. Enthusiasm becomes scope.** The first useful tool produces requests for five more: a money dashboard, a board report, reminders. In a small office each new program is another thing to own. *Mitigation:* a new program is built only when it has a named owner and a named first real use. The landing page has room for it; the organization may not.

**8. Shared computers, personal phones, old browsers.** A front-desk computer with one shared browser profile means Radar's "your own list" is the desk's list, and anyone at the desk can clear it. A coordinator will use a personal phone at events. *Mitigation:* the shared Volunteers & Events page is the right home for anything several people touch; Radar is personal by design and should stay on one person's own computer with its backup; everything has been tested at phone width.

**9. Privacy expectations without a law behind them.** No HIPAA applies, but a mental-health organization's volunteers and families expect discretion, and a public, no-sign-in site is not discreet. *Mitigation:* the rule in every guide (no health details, no background-check reports, no ID or bank details); yes/no plus a date for training and background checks and nothing more; Practice grants on the public Dashboard during testing; the sign-in on the volunteer list; phase two's locked door for anything financial.

**10. Turnover.** The person who learned it leaves; the next one inherits a link and no memory. *Mitigation:* the landing page is one address to put in the new-hire document, the welcome and tour run again for every new browser, and the guides are inside each program under Help. Nothing depends on remembering.

## A rollout that fits this office

Not all three programs to everyone at once. One program, one owner, one real use, two weeks each, overlapping where the owners differ.

| Weeks | Program | Owner | The one real use that counts as success |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Volunteers & Events | the volunteer coordinator | one real event run through it: jobs, names, who came, hours exported to a spreadsheet |
| 2–3 | Grant Dashboard | the grants person | one real grant with its reports and due dates, one marked done with its proof link |
| any time | Grant Radar | whoever scans for grants | two leads checked against the funder's page and one sent to the Dashboard |

Success is those three sentences, not a feature list. If they happen, phase two is worth doing. If they do not happen in four weeks, the tool is probably not the problem; the routine or the owner is, and the honest move is to fix that or stop, not to add features.

**The weekly rhythm, fifteen minutes total:** the owner of each program opens it once, marks what happened, and saves a backup. That is the whole maintenance load on NAMI in phase one.

## Signs to stop, and signs to go on

Stop, or re-scope, if after four weeks: records are still only examples; the owner says it is "fine" but cannot name a task it replaced; the same information is still kept somewhere else as well. Go on to phase two if: a report from the suite has been used for something real (a grant match, a board update, a thank-you list); the owner asks for a change rather than for permission to stop; a second person has started using the shared page unprompted.

## Changes the suite should consider because of this analysis

Not built. Each waits for a tester to confirm the need.

- **A printable sign-in sheet from an event page.** Large events still run on paper at the door; the sheet should come out of the program and the totals go back in. Today only the totals field exists.
- **A "for the board" page** on Volunteers & Events: this year's hours, events and gifts in four lines, ready to paste. The pieces exist on two pages; small offices need them in one.
- **An in-kind valuation line** on the hours report, using the standard hourly value of volunteer time, because that number goes into grant reports.
- **Spanish**, if any volunteers or families would use the programs directly. Nothing is translated today.
- **A one-line "what this replaces" note** on each landing-page card, so the double-entry decision in risk 2 is visible to everyone rather than agreed once and forgotten.

## What this costs

Money: nothing in phase one; nothing or a few dollars a month in phase two. The real costs are the builder's time, which is given, and staff attention, which is not free and is the thing this analysis is trying to protect. Every mitigation above is chosen to spend as little of it as possible.
